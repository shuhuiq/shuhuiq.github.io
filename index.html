my story

Most of my work comes back to one question: when can a system act on the signal it is given? In my PhD I trained multi-agent reinforcement learning policies to schedule large stochastic production networks, and at Samsung Display I shipped one — a scheduler for lines with hundreds of machines, where a reward or a constraint that is slightly wrong costs real money. Six of my patent applications are about exactly this: reweighting rewards, adapting a policy to a new reward, and letting a safety policy step in.

With LLM agents the question comes back in a harder form. Where a simulator or a design-rule checker can score the outcome, as in the chip-design environments my team builds, the reward can be trusted. Most agent tasks can only be partly checked, and the usual answer is to ask another model. In my own measurements, the LLM judges that agreed most with human labels still accepted plans that broke the user's budget — a shortcut an RL policy could learn to exploit. So I build verifiers that pass only what code can confirm (arithmetic, dates, quoted text) and say “cannot verify” otherwise.

The other thing I have learned is that a number is only as good as the way it was measured. I freeze held-out sets, write down what counts as a result before the run, measure once, and report the runs that did not work.

I am a Senior Staff ML Engineer and Senior Engineering Manager at Samsung Display American Lab in San Jose, and the project lead for three programs: LLM agents for display-chip design, AI-driven materials discovery, and multi-agent reinforcement learning (RL) for production scheduling. I lead a team of 8 researchers and engineers and own delivery to the design, materials and manufacturing teams.

  • Chip design. I built ChipEnv, a tool-use environment over the circuit and layout stack (circuit simulation, design-rule checking, parasitic RC extraction), generated agent trajectories in it, and fine-tuned LLMs to answer design questions and to reduce parasitic resistance and capacitance under layout rules.
  • Materials. I lead the search for long-lifetime deep-blue TADF emitters for OLED displays: constrained molecular generation, property predictors, molecular-dynamics simulation, RL-based candidate search and retrosynthesis filtering.
  • Scheduling. Our multi-agent RL scheduler runs on production lines with hundreds of machines and tens of thousands of orders a day, and cut operating cost by 20% against the previous scheduler.
  • Earlier. ChatYield, an LLM agent for yield optimization (fine-tuned Llama-3; every proposed action is tested in a digital-twin simulation before an engineer sees it), and production ML for predictive maintenance (100% recall at a 48-hour lead time) and defect inspection (99% recall, 95% of inspections automated).
2019 -

As an affiliated researcher at Stanford, I build Morpheus (morpheusplan.com), a second opinion for any AI-made plan. It checks every line of the plan against the user's rules, points to the exact line that breaks one and by how much, and offers only fixes that are checked again and keep the rest of the plan; when a fact is missing, it says “unclear” instead of guessing. The same checker reads each command a coding agent is about to run, inside Claude Code, Codex and Cursor, and stops the ones that break a rule. Morpheus grew out of a planning engine for science that builds a knowledge graph from papers (18,600+ actions from 665 documents, each traced to its page) and accepts a plan only after a verifier passes it. The research behind it: EMNLP Findings 2025 and four 2026 preprints. It is in an invite-only beta.

2026

I also build Honeyguide (formerly Rung; trial), a one-day shadow test for teams that pay for LLM APIs. It shows what a cheaper model setup would save, and what it would lose in quality, on the team's own traffic before they switch.

2026

And Trace Check-up, a small tool that reads an AI agent's log on your own computer. It says what the log is missing, whether there is enough of it, and how training compares with letting the agent try again — or that it can't tell yet, and what to collect.

bio

Shuhui Qu is a machine learning researcher and engineer working on reinforcement learning and on AI agents whose answers can be checked. At Samsung Display American Lab, Shuhui is a Senior Staff ML Engineer and the project lead for chip-design agents, materials discovery and multi-agent RL scheduling; at Stanford, Shuhui builds Morpheus, which checks an AI agent's plans and actions against the user's requirements before it acts (live at morpheusplan.com). Shuhui also builds Honeyguide and Trace Check-up. PhD, Stanford University. 50+ papers, 1,700+ citations, 10 granted US patents and more pending.
