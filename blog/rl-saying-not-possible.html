Can RL teach a model to say “not possible”?

October 2026

Ask an agent to plan something that cannot be done, and it rarely says so. It writes a plan that quietly breaks one of your limits, or it keeps searching until it runs out of tokens. I ran two small reinforcement learning (RL) experiments to see which reward teaches a model to say “not possible” when that is true, and only when that is true. The short answer: a reward can only teach what it can check.

The task

Each task asks for a one-day outing for a group: pick one option in each of four slots (transport, museum, lunch, evening), four options per slot, so 256 combinations. Every task has three limits: a total budget, a finishing time, and one excluded tag (“no option may contain nuts”). About a third of the tasks are impossible. Because the world is closed, a program can enumerate all 256 combinations, so I always know whether “not possible” is the right answer.

In both experiments the policy was trained with GRPO (a group of answers per task, each scored against the group average), with LoRA adapters on Tinker. The test set, the rewards and the reading rules were written down before training, and each test set was used once.

Experiment 1: a judge, a checker, and both

Qwen3-8B was trained for 20 steps (128 answers per step) under four rewards:

  • Judge. An LLM (gpt-6-luna) reads the task and the answer and decides. It can search the catalog itself, so it can tell whether “not possible” is correct.
  • Checker. Code checks only what the answer names. If the answer gives a plan, it can check the budget, the time and the excluded tag. It never searches the catalog, so it cannot confirm that no plan exists; for an answer of “not possible” it says “cannot verify”. Two versions: “cannot verify” scores 0, or −0.5.
  • Checker, then judge. The checker decides where it can; the judge decides the rest.

The judge taught the model to stop and say “not possible”; its answers also halved in length. The checker taught it nothing about refusing, while giving the best results on tasks that can be done. The reason is in what each reward pays. Under the checker, an honest “not possible” scores exactly the same as running out of tokens, since neither can be checked without a search, while a plan that breaks one of the three limits still earns +1/3. Nothing in that reward is worth more than admitting, so the model never moved toward it. OpenAI's Why language models hallucinate makes the same point about benchmarks: when “I don't know” scores zero and a guess sometimes scores, guessing always wins.

The judge had a price as well: on possible tasks the trained model said “not possible” more often (from 4 % to 7 %), usually after an arithmetic slip.

Experiment 2: make “not possible” checkable

If the checker cannot confirm a refusal, ask the model to prove it. The proof splits the options of one slot into cases and closes each case with a reason that can be checked from the catalog: a fixed option carries the excluded tag; or the cheapest allowed choice in every other slot already breaks the budget; or the quickest already finishes too late. A case can be split again on a second slot. Checking a line means scanning four options per slot, never the 256 combinations. I tested that the checker is sound: on possible tasks, no proof — random, or the strongest honest attempt — is ever accepted. The reward: +1 for a proof whose every line is true and whose cases cover everything, −1 for any false line, 0 for an incomplete proof. The control reward gives every “not possible” 0.

The first model could not do it. Qwen3-8B wrote no valid proof in 72 answers to impossible tasks, so the +1 never appears and RL has nothing to strengthen. I switched to Inkling-Small, a reasoning model, and trained its reasoning tokens as well.

The second model was already honest. Before any training, Inkling-Small said “not possible” on about 90 % of impossible tasks and never on a possible one. What it lacked was correct evidence. Here is one task (9 people, start 13:00, budget $665, finish by 17:30, no nuts), where the conclusion is right and one line is false:

FINAL PLAN: NOT POSSIBLE — no allowed combination satisfies both the $665 budget and the 17:30 deadline.

M2 is the slowest museum (145 minutes), not the quickest. With the quickest museum and lunch, T3 + M1 + L1 + E3 takes 260 minutes and ends at 17:20, in time. The conclusion survives only because that combination costs $828. On the test set, about one “not possible” in five came with a proof that checked out, and about one in three contained a false step — most often an option called “cheapest” or “quickest” that was not. A grader that checks only the conclusion would mark all of these correct.

My first checker was wrong in a different way. It was strict about notation and accepted none of the 68 proofs Inkling-Small wrote on the development tasks. A checker with the same soundness that tolerates notation — a slot letter instead of its name, the arithmetic shown on the line, cases nested one level deeper — accepted 15 of them. Trained against the strict one, RL would have taught a notation, and I would have reported that the model cannot prove infeasibility.

Ten steps of RL changed nothing. Both rewards, 10 steps each, tested once on 60 new tasks (4 answers per task):

The reward differed between answers in up to half of the task groups each step, so there was a learning signal, and the model did not move. On the training samples the share of proofs with a false step even rose, from 29 % to 36 %. With 10 steps and one seed I cannot tell “too little training” from “this reward does not teach it”.

What I take from this

  1. A reward teaches only what it can check. A checker that cannot confirm “impossible” pays an honest refusal the same as giving up, and RL learns neither to stop nor to admit.
  2. A checker that is strict about format trains format. Before training, find out what the checker rejects that is actually correct.
  3. Check the evidence, not only the conclusion. A model can be right for the wrong reasons a third of the time, and a conclusion-only grader will never see it.
  4. Measure the base model first. The behaviour I set out to teach was already there in the stronger model; the gap was somewhere else.

Limits of these results: a small closed world, English only, one training seed per reward, 10–20 steps, about $50 of compute in total. The experiments were pre-registered, and both negative results are reported as measured.
