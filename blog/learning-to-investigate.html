We tried to build a scientific agent that learns from experience. Here is what failed.

November 2026 · research note · Learning to Investigate

Over two studies, we tested whether an LLM agent can learn from its own experiments and notice when a model it was given is wrong. Mostly it could not. The ways it failed are specific and measurable, and three of them changed how we think about scientific agents.

  • one model: Claude Opus 5.5
  • synthetic worlds, 6 inputs
  • 10 noisy observations per episode
  • main tests registered before they ran

The setup

Each world is a hidden function of six knobs with Gaussian noise. Only one to three knobs matter, combined in a specific way: a threshold, a product, a narrow peak. The agent sees two observations, then chooses eight more experiments one at a time. With those ten points it has to do its job, and simple statistical tools get the same ten points for comparison.

Before each main test we wrote down the comparison and the rule for calling it a success. We report the result of that rule even when we did not like it.

Finding 1

More experience ≠ better decisions

In the first study, the agent wrote lessons from its own earlier experiments: 5, 20, or 60 past episodes, with feedback on how informative each design had been. We then gave the lessons back to it and scored its new designs with a fixed Bayesian analyzer. The score is the log probability of the true structure after ten points, in nats. Zero is perfect.

On familiar mechanisms, lessons improved the design score by 0.3 to 0.6 nats. On new mechanisms we found no reliable gain. That is a lack of evidence, not proof that transfer is impossible: the intervals still allow small gains.

The dots matter more than the bars. Three texts written from the same 60 episodes scored −1.47, −1.71, and −2.64 on new mechanisms. Our first transfer test happened to use the best of them, and it looked like transfer (+0.42 over no experience). When we sampled three texts per condition, the gain disappeared. One extracted text is one draw from a noisy process, and it is not enough to support a claim.

One lesson that made the agent worse

The three worst texts on new mechanisms shared one piece of advice: start from a corner with every knob at its minimum, then change one knob at a time. In six inputs this uses most of the budget, and it never sets two knobs high together. A mechanism that switches on only when two knobs are both high stays invisible.

So we ran a registered intervention. We took the two extracted texts that contained this corner lesson and made five versions of each: the original, the lesson deleted, the lesson narrowed in scope, the lesson replaced by neutral text of the same length, and a different lesson deleted. That gave 300 episodes on new mechanisms.

Deleting the corner lesson improved the design score by +1.02 [+0.65, +1.41] nats. Both texts agreed (+1.06 and +0.98). Deleting a different lesson of similar length did not help, while replacing the corner lesson with neutral text helped as much as deleting it. So the content of that lesson caused the loss, not the length of the text.

The behavior change is easy to see. With the lesson, about six of eight experiments varied one knob at a time. Without it, about one did, and six to seven experiments set several knobs high together.

What this does not show

  • We chose these two texts after seeing their scores, and we tested them on the worlds where we first saw the pattern.
  • We deleted the lesson. The agent did not find the harmful lesson itself.
  • One lesson in two texts is not a general mechanism of negative transfer. It shows that a single sentence of procedural advice can redirect an agent's experiments and cost it about one nat of information.

Finding 2

Naming the variables ≠ writing the mechanism

The second study asked a different question. We gave the agent a family of nine formulas “from earlier work” and told it the family might be incomplete. In half the worlds the true mechanism was outside the family. The agent had to judge whether the family was adequate, give a probability, and submit its own formula. This test ran on 60 fresh worlds, twice each, with three registered claims.

The agent's judgement was no better than a one-line misfit test on the same data. The interval was too wide to call them equal either (+0.001 [−0.081, +0.088]). Combining the agent's probability with the statistic added nothing (+0.003).

Its formulas depend on the type of mechanism

Pooled over all wrong-family worlds, the agent's predictions were no better than ridge regression on the same ten points (+0.035 [−0.092, +0.165], inconclusive). That average hides two opposite results. This split by mechanism is a secondary analysis, with 20 episodes per family.

On step, a threshold on one knob, the agent was clearly better than ridge (−0.16). It wrote a threshold, which a polynomial model cannot express. On three_way, a product of three knobs, it was clearly worse (+0.34). Ridge has that product among its terms. The agent rarely wrote it, and 15 of its 20 formulas predicted worse than a constant. So the agent is not uniformly bad at discovering mechanisms. It has a strong grip on some structures and almost none on others.

It names three knobs and writes two

On three_way, the agent named all three relevant knobs in 0.45 of episodes, tried their product in a fit in 0.20, and submitted it in 0.15. The registered gap between naming and constructing was +0.30 [+0.10, +0.45]. In most episodes the product never became a candidate at all. It was never tried and then rejected.

The data was not the obstacle. On the pilot worlds, fitting the true form to the agent's own ten points gave a prediction error of 0.16, against 0.74 for the agent's formula. On sum_gate the pattern repeats: the agent named both knobs in 0.55 of episodes but wrote a threshold on their sum in 0.05.

How to read the naming result

Naming three knobs is a low bar. Our rule only checks that the names appear in the sentence, so it is not evidence that the agent understands a three-way interaction. The test is also small: ten worlds, run twice each. It passed our registered rule, and it needs a larger replication.

Finding 3

More hypotheses ≠ better science

If the agent names the right knobs but writes the wrong formula, maybe a simple tool can finish the job. We wrote fixed rules that read the agent's words: the knobs it names and cue words such as “interaction” or “threshold”. The rules generate eight candidate formulas from a small grammar, and BIC picks one. We compared this with a generic search that ignores the words. That search takes the knobs from a ridge screen of the data and draws eight candidates at random from the same grammar. This used existing pilot records only. It was development data, with no new agent runs.

Language-guided expansion did not beat the generic search. Its prediction error was −0.029 lower, against the −0.05 we required in advance, so we stopped there. Its one gain, on three_way, came from the word “interaction” pushing the product up the list. Searching the whole grammar without any words got most of that gain.

We then checked whether a better selection rule fixes this. On 100 new offline worlds, neither AICc nor leave-one-out cross-validation reduced the loss without many more false revisions. Leave-one-out cut the loss by 0.05 but revised +0.28 more of the worlds that needed no revision. Two caveats apply. Those worlds used a fixed space-filling design, and on the agent's own designs selection failed less often (0.25 against 0.58). Also, the best candidate is identified with held-out data that no selector can see, so part of that gap is unavoidable.

The picture is consistent. With ten noisy points, the correct structure often reaches the candidate list and still loses to a wrong one. Generating more candidates does not change that. Better evidence might.

How we kept ourselves honest

  • Rules first. Every main test had its comparison and pass rule written down before it ran, and changes after a review were recorded before any affected run.
  • Sample the text, not just the episodes. One lesson text misled us early. After that, each condition had three independently extracted texts.
  • Strong, simple baselines. A chi-square test, ridge regression, and a generic grammar search saw the same data as the agent. In no pooled comparison was the agent clearly better than them.
  • Stop when the rule says stop. Four directions ended on a pre-set rule: scaling experience, choosing between design strategies, language-guided expansion, and a better selection criterion.

One stop is worth a sentence. Before building an agent that chooses between design strategies, we scripted the strategies and measured the best possible choice. Perfect selection would have gained only +0.24 nats against the 0.5 we required. One fixed design was best on seven of eight families, so we built nothing.

What we would test next

None of the following has been tested. The agent did not lack ideas. It lacked a reliable way to judge which past lessons and which current explanation still deserved trust. Two directions follow from that.

  • An agent that notices when its ten points cannot separate its candidate explanations, and chooses the next experiment to separate them.
  • Lessons that carry a tested scope, and an agent that checks a lesson against new evidence before following it. In our narrowed version, the scope and the budget changed together, so we cannot say yet whether written conditions help.

Limits

  • One model, one effort level, one prompt frame. Other models may behave differently.
  • Synthetic worlds with six knobs and eight mechanism types. Real systems are messier.
  • Known Gaussian noise, which helps every statistical baseline here.
  • A fixed budget of ten points. More data could change detection, transfer, and selection.
  • Restricted formula families and grammars on every side of each comparison.
  • In the first study, the new-mechanism worlds were used more than once. In the second study, the correct-family worlds were half peak, and the false-alarm rate (0.27) was higher than in the pilot (0.15).
