Train, or let it try again? What your agent's own logs can already tell you

October 2026

A team runs an AI agent that uses tools: it looks up orders, sends emails, updates records. On some tasks it fails. There are two common ways to make it do better. You can train a smaller model on the agent's own conversations, or you can let the agent try the task again. Both cost money. Training costs once, up front, and can go either way. Trying again costs every time, and it works only if the agent can tell that a try failed. Most teams choose on a hunch.

I built a small tool, Trace Check-up, that answers from the log the agent already writes, on your own computer, before anything is trained. An earlier post, Before you train on your agent's logs, lists the mistakes it was built to catch. This one is about the three questions it answers, and what it says when it can't answer them.

1. What to record

A log can only answer what it records. The check-up looks for eleven things and marks each one recorded, partly recorded or missing. Among them: every action and what came back, exactly as it was, errors and empty answers too; a task name that is the same every time the same kind of job is done; whether each conversation worked, and who decided that; and the tokens each one used. The example report shows the whole list.

Two of these matter more than they look. Without a task name, tries at the same job can't be grouped, so nothing can be said about trying again. Without “worked or failed”, nothing can be said about training. And “who decided” matters too: the agent's own word that it is done is not a check.

2. How much is enough

Two counts. The first is each task tried at least 8 times, each marked worked or failed. That shows how much trying again already fixes, and trying again needs no training, so training has to do better than that to be worth it.

The second is conversations that worked, and say who decided they worked: an automatic check, a person, or a system other than the agent, such as your order database. The report draws its line at about 5,000. Below that, published results go both ways. In one study, the same cold-start fine-tuning on about 2,000 trajectories took Qwen3-8B from 41.0 to 31.4 on a retail tool benchmark, and Qwen3-14B from 43.1 to 53.7 (MUA-RL, Table 1). The 5,000 line is a rough guide, not a measured minimum: no study I know of has measured it for small models on customer-service or CRM-style tool tasks. And many different tasks tell you more than many copies of a few.

3. Train, or try again, counted in tries

The report puts two cards side by side. If the agent tries again: the share of tasks that worked on the first try and within 2, 4 and 8 tries, measured on your log. If you train a small model now: a rough range from published studies, marked low confidence, with each study listed.

They are weighed at the same cost. If the agent needs on average a few tries to get a task right, one try of a trained model is weighed against that many tries of the untrained one. If the log records tokens, the report also says how many tokens one try uses. Today the comparison is counted in tries.

What it reads, and what it refuses to guess

It reads only the file you name, never changes it, and makes no network or API call. Each sentence on the page is a phrase with numbers from your log filled in, chosen by written rules. “Where this comes from” under each check and card names the reading of your log or the paper it rests on, and the limit it used. Every limit is listed on the page and can be changed.

When the log can't decide, it says “can't tell yet” and what would change that. It does not draw a guess. Two conditions for trying again are often not in a log: whether your system can tell, as the agent runs, that a try failed, and whether the agent's actions are safe to do twice (a refund paid twice is not). The report shows what your log suggests about each, and asks you to confirm what it can't see.

What it says on a made-up log

The tool comes with a made-up support log: 16 conversations about 2 tasks, refunding an order and changing an address, each tried 8 times, 32 actions in all. Here is the full report on it. Its numbers show how the report reads; they say nothing about any real agent.

  • What to record: 10 of the 11 things are recorded. The optional copy of the records is not.
  • The first thing to fix: “Check the made-up IDs before training the AI.” In 4 of the 16 places where the agent used an ID, nobody had given it and it had not looked it up. Training on those conversations would teach a model to make IDs up.
  • Trying again: the curve looks good. 69 % of tasks worked on the first try, 93 % within 2 tries, and 100 % within 4 or 8.
  • And yet both cards say “can't tell yet”. The curve stands on 2 tasks, and the report wants at least 12 before it says what trying again would do. The training card has 11 conversations that worked; even with about 2,000, published studies saw training go either way. The next step it gives is concrete: 10 more tasks, each tried 8 times, and about 4,989 more conversations that worked.
  • A question back: at least 34 % of the actions that worked change records, most often refunds. So before it counts on trying again, it asks whether those actions are safe to repeat.

That is the point of the example. A curve that reaches 100 % on two tasks is not evidence, and both cards say so: can't tell yet.

Why a small tool on your own computer

Agent logs hold customers' names, orders and messages. The simplest way to keep them private is not to send them anywhere, so the tool runs on your computer, offline, on the CPU. On the made-up log the whole report took under a second on a laptop.

The answer also must not depend on who sells training. No training platform pays me, the advice includes “don't train”, and there is no service behind the tool to sell. It does not promise that training will help.

Can I run it?

Not yet: the code is not public. The example report is the page the tool writes, and the product page lists the log formats it reads.

What it does not do yet

  • Compare a trained agent with the untrained one from two logs, at the same cost and on the same tasks. It is designed and not built.
  • Pick the conversations to train on. Today it shows which worked and which had problems.
  • Count who decided a success in a table export. It says so on the page.
  • Train anything, or give a single-number forecast. It gives directions and ranges, and “can't tell yet”.

Limits: the checks were built on one project's own logs and mistakes; another team's logs will break assumptions mine did not. The training card rests on published studies that go both ways, and is labelled low confidence for that reason.
