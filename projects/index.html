Morpheus · for teams that let AI agents plan and act

Know which of your requirements an agent breaks — before it acts.

Morpheus checks every step an AI agent plans or takes against the requirements you gave it, and tells you, for each requirement, whether it is met, broken, or cannot be checked — with the evidence.

  • Check. Your requirements are kept across the whole conversation, and a verdict rests only on what code can confirm — the plan's own “total: within budget” never counts. When it cannot tell, it says so instead of guessing. On a blind test it let none of 8 planted budget overruns through (2 flagged as broken, 6 as cannot-check).
  • Stop. Inside Claude Code, Codex and Cursor it blocks the tool call that would break a rule you set — a new dependency, a force push, an edit to the file that computes your metric — and checks the agent's “done” before you believe it. Replayed on 4,750 real tool calls: 10 blocks, all correct.
  • Fix. When a step breaks a requirement, it proposes alternatives at that step and re-checks them before you see them.
  • Record. For ML experiment loops, it turns a run into a record anyone can re-verify: a frozen evaluator, the noise across seeds, and an audit for metric gaming.

Try it: morpheusplan.com is the web product: a second opinion for any AI-made plan — from ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, a friend or your own notes. It checks every line against your rules, points to the exact line that breaks one and by how much, and offers only fixes that are checked again and keep the rest of the plan. Invite-only beta, free during the beta. In planted-mistake tests the default check found 19–20 of 20 hidden mistakes and called no broken plan fine. With simulated users, 9 of 10 tasks ended with every rule met, no broken rule was marked met by the plan's own numbers, and a task cost $0.116 (internal tests, October 2026).

Where it came from: a planning engine for science (Morphism-Oriented Reasoning with Provable, Honest, Epistemic Understanding for Science) that builds a knowledge graph from papers — 18,600+ actions from 665 documents, every one traced to its page — plans over it, and accepts a plan only after a verifier passes it. Research: EMNLP Findings 2025 and four 2026 preprints. Honeyguide (formerly Rung) · for teams paying for LLM APIs

See the saving and the quality cost before you switch to a cheaper model.

A one-day shadow test on your own traffic. All day, your users get the top model's answers (in the hosted trial, DeepSeek's pro-max, on our key and at our cost). Behind each request, Honeyguide records what a cheap-model-first setup would have answered and what it would have cost. You mark about 30 answers, and the report says whether a cheaper setup is enough for your traffic.

  • Cheap model first, checked. The cheap model answers first, a check decides what goes out, and a failed answer climbs to a stronger model, under a hard cap per request. Our test: 76% cheaper than always using the top model, for 8 points of quality ($1.86 against $7.75 per 1,000 requests; 69% against 77% right).
  • The report picks the setup. Sometimes the answer is one cheaper model, not a cascade. On the same test one cheaper model got 73% right at $1.43 per 1,000 requests — better than the cascade on both counts.
  • One URL to change. It uses the OpenAI API format: point your client at it for the day.

Try it: honeyguide-trial.onrender.com. The trial is invite-only; applications are open, and no team has run a shadow day yet. Honeyguide is open source: the code is published when the first team applies.

Our test: 100 short RouterArena questions on DeepSeek models, September 2026, not reviewed by anyone outside. Write-ups: Can a cheaper model do your work? · Don't predict, verify (as Rung) · related work: routers and cascades.

Trace Check-up · for teams running AI agents

Before you train your agent, find out if it's worth it.

It reads your agent's log on your own computer. In about a minute it says what the log is missing, whether there is enough of it, and how training compares with letting the agent try again, counted in tries — or that it can't tell yet, and what to collect.

  • Train, or try again? Two cards side by side: what trying again does, measured on your own log, and a rough range for training a small model, with the published results it is based on. Either card can say “can't tell yet”.
  • Do you have enough? Tasks tried at least 8 times, conversations that worked and who decided they worked, tasks that never worked — and how many more to collect.
  • What to record. The eleven things a log needs before it can answer, and the ones yours is missing.
  • Honest by construction. Every sentence points to a reading of your log or a cited paper, and it says “can't tell” when the log cannot decide. It never promises that training will help, and no training platform pays us, so the advice does not depend on which way you go.

Status: a small tool that runs on your own computer, so your log never leaves it. It is open source: the code is published when the first team asks for it; until then the example report (on a made-up log) shows what it makes. Comparing a trained agent with the untrained one at the same cost is not built yet. Product page.
